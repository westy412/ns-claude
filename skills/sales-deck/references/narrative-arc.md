# Narrative Arc: structure, grounding, safety

A CEO's attention is held by a story with stakes, not a feature tour. Build the
deck as a contrast arc that leads to one decision.

## The spine (~12–15 slides)

```
1  Cover                 brand, the one-line framing, audience/date
2  The moment            the shift that resets the board (problem setup)
3  The problem           where the client stands today (a stark stat helps)
4  The bet               the insight / recommendation (state it EARLY)
5  The outcome           what winning looks like (often the flywheel/diagram-hero)
6  The solution          one platform / N worlds (the map; grid archetype)
7..  Each world          one slide per domain (flanked or menu archetype)
N-3 Proof                it's not theory (quote + validation)
N-2 Economics            new money / leaner base (stat-forward)
N-1 The path             how we get there safely (steps)
N   The close            the decision, stated plainly (never "thank you")
```

This is Duarte's "what is / what could be" contrast and McKinsey's SCQA
(Situation, Complication, Question, Answer): the first ~4 slides create the
question in the CEO's mind, and the recommendation (the bet) answers it early.
Everything after builds conviction for a decision they already see coming.

## Principles

- **Lead the recommendation by ~slide 4.** Don't bury it past the midpoint.
- **One world per slide.** If the product has four domains, that's four slides,
  parallel in layout (consistent is good for a parallel series), distinct in
  accent colour.
- **End on the decision, not gratitude.** The last slide is the call to action
  or the one vivid line. No "thank you" / "questions?" slide.
- **The ask can be soft.** If the explicit ask (money, mandate, scope) isn't
  settled, keep the close to the decision framing and put the candidate asks in
  the speaker notes for the presenter to choose. Do not invent a hard number.

## Source grounding (critical)

Build every slide from the client's own material (vault, transcripts, docs).

- **Only assert what was actually discussed.** Ideas that an AI or a teammate
  "expanded" but the client never raised do not go on the slide — the presenter
  would have to defend them live. When in doubt, cut it or move it to notes.
- **Trace claims.** Numbers, quotes, and named features should come from the
  source. If you can't source it, soften or drop it.
- **Prefer the client's own words** for the killer lines (a quote from their CEO
  back at them lands harder than your phrasing).

## Sensitivity

Some true facts are politically loaded (headcount reduction, spend, positioning
against a parent/group, layoffs implied by "run it with ~50 people").

- Keep the **number** if it's the point, but **frame it as leverage**, not cuts.
- Put the **caution in the notes** (`.flag`), so the presenter calibrates to the
  room rather than the slide forcing the issue.
- Pricing/spend specifics: keep high-level on-slide; defer details to a
  follow-up. Flag "if asked for figures, don't commit live" in notes.

## Audience + presenter shape the deck

- **Altitude** follows the audience. A Group CEO wants the bet, the proof, the
  money, and the decision, fast — not implementation detail.
- **Cue cards are written for the named presenter.** If it's not the founder,
  the notes carry the context the founder would have had in their head.
